Portfolio

QuestRex Backstoryr

Role: Full Stack(ish) Developer
Tools: React, xAI, Wordpress
Link: https://questrex.biz/backstoryr/

The QuestRex Backstoryr is my first foray into coding agentic, AI-generated content and it turned out quite well. Originally I built it using plain JavaScript, but was unhappy with the results. Yes, it could fling out tons of random notions, but none of them meshed together into anything sensible (kinda like early Beck lyrics). And even with massive random tables, the options were still limited. When the modern batch of LLMs arrived I attempted to do the same with them but the results were equally disappointing. Without detailed, carefully structured prompts the results were too chaotic to be useful and spending the time to create those prompts for each character was more effort that it was worth.

I realized that combining the two approaches could work, and it does. The front-end allows users to quickly define key traits of their character, which the app then passes to the AI to generate a cohesive backstory. The results are displayed on the UI, where they can be refined further. I think the outcome is pretty useful.

The agentic programming model is something I plan to explore more. While modern AI is powerful, to get solid results, you often need to narrow the scope and define the task clearly. It takes effort to fine-tune prompts and model parameters to get useful output.

urAtlas Odyssey

Role: Full Stack(ish) Developer
Tools: React, SQLite, Express.js, Firebase Auth, Cursor, Tawk

This is my first full-stack application where I was in charge of every pixel and bit of code on the page (although I'll admit to leaning mightily on AI to help with the backend, which is not my forte).

The program is a riff on the popular 12 Week Year concept, bringing clarity and intensity to a task so it can be completed in record time. It helps users do that by setting meaningful goals, tracking weekly execution, and getting visual feedback via momentum indicators, check-in dashboards, and self-assigned scoring.

The UX was far more challenging than I expected, especially since I had to architect it with enough flexibility to support both life coach and AI integration in future iterations.

I'm not making the URL public to protect the data I'm collecting from a discrete group of life coaches who are currently testing it. If you're a prospective employer and want to take a look, just ask as I'm happy to share.

QuestRex Character Generator

Role: UX / Front-End Specialist
Tools: React.js, SASS
Link: https://questrex.biz/character-builder/

This is a complex character generator I built to support my tabletop role-playing game, QuestRex. For all the nostalgia about the "good old days" of simpler JavaScript, I can't imagine building this in anything other than React. There's just SO much state to manage it would've been a nightmare of spaghetti code in POJS. I designed and coded most of it myself, but my good friend Frank Ali helped a lot with certain sections.

There's no database or backend here. To avoid that complexity and to keep it as modular as possible I store everything in plain JSON or Markdown files, which are parsed directly into the page. Clunky? Well... maybe a little. But more often than not, the KISS principle (Keep It Simple, Stupid) wins over being devastatingly clever.

Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC)

Role: Senior UX / Front-End Developer @ Publicis Sapient
Tools: Axure, React.js, SASS, Photophop, Illustrator, Accessibility Standards

This was my last corporate gig and a great one because it finally gave me the chance to land in the UX/front-end development space I'd been tacking towards for years. For a while, tech roles had become so siloed and specialized that holding a more holistic position was nearly impossible. But this one let me bounce between building wireframes, giving client presentations, jumping into front-end Jira tickets, and even designing a few logos. It was all very satisfying.

The application itself was completely new and based on some rather fuzzy internal processes the client had. We had to extract their actual needs and pivot quickly when new requirements surfaced (which they did frequently). Like so many clients I've worked with, they knew exactly how to do their job but had a much harder time articulating the specific steps and exceptions within their workflows. In the end they got not only a great app but also a much deeper understanding of those steps and processes which, honestly, might've been the most valuable part.

HRSA Health Workforce Connector

Role: Senior Front-End Developer with some UX
Tools: Angular, CSS, SASS, Accessibility Standards, Axure

For this project, I was primarily a front-end developer working in Angular as part of a very large team. Toward the end, I got to do some wireframing, but it remained mostly a coding-heavy role and I hardly did any designing at all.

The coding was a bear. We started with the beta version of Angular, had to recode the entire thing when 1.0 was released, and then reworked large swaths repeatedly until Angular finally settled into a stable form. Launching a major project with a novel technology was, perhaps, an ill-advised decision from the higher-ups. But with perseverance and the grace of Stack Overflow we pulled it off.

By the time I rolled off, the app was motoring along smoothly. The project crises made for very rapid learning. It was also one of the largest teams I've ever worked on (100+ people). Towards its end I was working with the UX team to create style conventions across the various sub-apps and that was a satisfying close to the ride.

PenFed Credit Union Mobile App

Role: Senior UX / Front-End Developer
Tools: Hybrid Mobile Frameworks, Secure Front-End Patterns

This was a mobile banking application for Pentagon Federal Credit Union, built as a hybrid web app for deployment on both Android and iOS. I worked on the front end and was largely responsible for the CSS and styling.

That task turned out to be far more complicated than expected, thanks to the peculiarities of various mobile OSes and browsers. Font rendering, layout, and z-index issues were my bane - particularly on older browsers. I remember a teammate joking that it would've been cheaper to just buy complaining users new phones than spend days figuring out why something wasn't rendering correctly.

Still, we pulled it off, and the final product was solid. Nowadays most mobile dev has moved away from hybrid apps as they just introduce too many limitations. Native apps offer far more control and consistency.

Department of Energy HR Website

Role: Lead UX / Senior Front-End Developer @ DST / SDSE
Tools: HTML, CSS, JavaScript, Accessibility

This was a complete redesign of the Department of Energy's HR office. I knew I was in uncharted territory when I was directed to rename the site to "The Office of the Chief Human Capital Officer." Just rolls off the tongue, right?

Bureaucratic tape aside, this job was mostly an exercise in getting a large number of stakeholders to agree on what actually needed to be built. This remains my peak people-skills project, and in the end, they served me well. Everyone agreed. Everyone liked what was built. And no one saw a problem with "The Office of the Chief Human Capital Officer.". Mission accomplished.

Fun project story: While waiting to show my designs to a director for sign-off, I sat outside his office listening to him gushing about a great new company that was going to revolutionize solar energy. The company? Solyndra.

NREPP Online Training Course

Role: Lead UX / Front-End Developer @ MANILA Consulting Group
Tools: HTML, CSS, JavaScript, Graphics, WebMerge

Of all the work I've done over the years, this was probably my favorite project. It was a large, interactive course for the National Registry of Evidence-based Programs and Practices (NREPP).

We had a team of PhDs designing the course, writers and editors shaping the copy, and then me (and my small team) turning it into a working website. I got to do all the design and most of the coding. It was pure creative control, which is a rare thing.

When we learned the course had to be usable on a CD-ROM (those were the days, eh?), I discovered the magical WebMerge tool from 4th World Software. It let us "mail-merge" our database-driven course into a static, portable site that could run off a disc. I still a fan of Webmerge and wager that half of all sites built today would work better with this approach.

I think I loved doing this project because I got to be the captain of all things Web. I chose the tech stack, implemented the majority of the code, designed the interface, and handled the graphics. This was right before websites became too complicated for one person to field it all themselves. Interestingly, with the new AI assisted coding we're kind of circling back to that paradigm again.

The site was taken down years ago but I still have a copy and have toyed with the idea of posting it somewhere for nostalgia's sake, but I'm not sure about the legality. So for now, it lives on in memory and maybe a backup drive or two.
